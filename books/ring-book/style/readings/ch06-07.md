# Close reading — chapters 6 and 7, "Juggling with scopes" and "Functions, objects and beyond"

Read 2026-10-11 by a fresh reader (an agent given the extracted text and `STYLE-BRIEF.md`, nothing else). Both extractions have dropped words mid-sentence; treated as extraction damage, not style.

**1. Shape of a chapter**

Openings differ. ch06 opens abstract-then-conflict: ¶1 defines where variables live ("they depend on the scope they are living in, or moving in if you want"); ¶2 a one-line definition; ¶3 stages the conflict as a question ("what is the value to be considered by Ring: you or me?!"). ch07 opens with a quoted stranger's forum post, then answers it as a letter ("You are not alone, my friend"), then widens to the industry ("the comeback of functions, ported by ubiquitous JavaScript"). Both openings end with a promise plus a boast: ch06 "say hello to the haven of unsolvable mistakes and hear-pulling program errors"; ch07 "Welcome to one of the most instructive chapters of the entire book."

Sections are built as a chain of tiny runnable files: a 2–12 line listing, "Execute", the exact output or error message, then one or two paragraphs decoding the output line by line ("§ Object scope": "here are three portions in the output we can describe as the following"). Code is roughly 40–50% of ch06 and over half of ch07; listings in ch07's OOP half grow to 30–40 lines (bank example, natural-language parser). Code comes *before* explanation almost always; the explanation reads the output back.

No fixed closing apparatus: no summary, no exercise block, no "what you learned". ch06 closes on a tool (the debugger) with a modest sentence: "So obvious for a little program, but all the benefits ... when you are programming in the large". ch07 closes on an exhortation: "create your mother-tong programming language by just translating Ring keywords and operators!" Exercises appear ad hoc inside sections ("§ etween and": "I'll leave this kind of conflict to you as an exercise"; "§ Managing a collection of objects": "As a homework, I want you to add an apply(f) method"). Every section ends with a bridge sentence to the next ("§ Comparing functions": "from a programming garden full of function-flowers, to a programming by object-stars…").

**2. The teaching moves**

The dominant sequence is *make it fail first*: "§ Global scope" starts with a one-word program `me`, "Ring complains and says:", then the fix. "§ Recursion": "it will execute infinitely… until all the available memory ... is completely consumed. Let's make it happen!" The Guffa walkthrough in ch07 is this move stretched over a page: nine successive compiler errors, each read as a hint ("Ring indicates where to start", "Same recipe"). Naming comes after showing: `from` is used in code, then "In OOP jargon, we say that Customer inherits from Person." Definitions, when given, are one sentence and plain ("A class is the blueprint for an object"; "In one sentence: there is a conflict between two scopes when they both contain variables of the same name").

Likely confusion is voiced for the reader and answered: "You would argue that y was defined in line 14 ... You are right but these are two different and intangible local scopes" (§ Object scope); "You may be a little bit surprised! Do you expected to see programmer not mansour?"; "At this level, two legitimate questions can be asked" (§ Sending parameters). Then a rule in bold-register: "The rule here is that:", "A rule here:", "his is the rule you should retain".

Limits of the language are admitted but reframed as design choice: "which is revolutionary, but uncommon, and somehow risky!" (opening); "Other programming languages don't allow because breaking the program security. But Ring allows us ... programmers should benefit responsibly" (§ Object scope); FP cons are listed honestly ("Recursion ... is not that natural, yet less expressive than simply using a plain old for/next loop"). He stops short openly: "I won't go further here in implementing the lacking functions"; "I let you dig the subject in Ring documentation" (three times in ch07).

**3. Voice and address**

"You" is the reader at the keyboard, "we" the pair reading the output, "I" rare and reserved for personal fact or opinion: "In my case, it was:"; "Personally, I would just use @"; "Here in Sousse, where I live"; "Believe me: this is a key differentiator of Ring". Imperatives are the spine: Execute/Run/Save appear ~37 times across the two chapters ("Save, run, and see:", "Try it and run the program again"). Direct questions end sections as a nudge: "What do you think?", "You see?", "Interested? You can't say no.", "Very clear, isn't it?", "A totally different programming experience, isn't it?" (~39 prose question lines). Prose exclamations are frequent (~39 lines): "Wow, we're done: no errors at all!", "Yes, just me!". Humour is small and warm, often at the author's own expense or via his sample data: "meet your (and my) favorite job worldwide: programmer!"; "a beautiful NULL"; "Do you you loved Ring? So happy for that."; "Who said that a dynamic programming languages, like Ring, was a bad thing?!". Grave register appears only when the subject is design ethics: encapsulation, "By law, the bank is constrained...".

**4. The images**

Metaphors are plentiful, home-made, and mostly dropped after one sentence: scopes as weather ("a source of rain of errors and thunderstorms!"), debugger as "your spy's eye", "slow down the time machine", eval() as "the horse of all functions in Ring. Here we ride the horse…", recursion as consuming memory, objects as citizens with houses ("they enter from the door and not the window!", "no one in the city"), program as "a theater", functions as verbs/objects as nouns. Two carry real weight: the house/door/window figure in "§ Securing the object world with encapsulation" does the explaining that no code there does; and the Guffa (Sousse, couscous, a palm-frond bag, "cultural reasons" if he moved to Amsterdam) is sustained across two sections and becomes the running code example. Sample data is personal: `me = "Mansour"`, `age = "43"`, Kalidia, Ahmed, Mahmoud, Sara, "Egypt", "I love you, Ring!", "01/08/1976". Ring's author is named as a character ("travelling in the brain of Mahmoud Fayed"). No Ringoria/map cast in these two chapters.

**5. Sentence and paragraph craft**

Rhythm: a long explanatory sentence, then a short verdict sentence ("It works, but with a serious design problem."; "So fine, but at what cost?!"; "As simple as this."). One-sentence paragraphs are common as transitions ("This said, we are ready to start!"). Favourite openers/connectives, counted over both chapters: "Let's" 45; "Execute/Run" ~37; "Therefore/Nevertheless/Hence/By analogy" 23; "In fact" 14 (ch07 10, ch06 4); "Of course" 9; "First/Second/Finally" enumerations 9; "Now," 7; interjections "Yes/Ok/Wow/Yep/Seriously" 7; "hole" for whole 10 and "staff" for stuff 3 (recurring misspellings); "just/simply/very" 43. Punctuation: colon before output ("Ring answers:"), trailing ellipsis to hand over to code ("…then the Ring moves from the global scope"), "?!" combination four times, parentheses for asides ("(or attribute if you prefer)"). Code names appear bare in prose, with () for functions and [] for lists (show(), aPerson[]).

Tics an editor would cut: French-shaped constructions ("Ring obeys to", "a useful information", "It's normal then that Ring can't capture it"); superlatives ("best of the best friend", "two world-class innovative paradigms", "one of the most instructive chapters"); over-long sentences (§ opening ch07, ¶2 is one 80-word sentence); "the so called" twice; "very" used in code comments and prose ("it's very well commented").

**6. Structure of explanation**

Concrete-to-general every time: particular output → "The rule here is". Principles are extracted after, never stated first, with one exception (FP "Pros. Three of them / Cons. Three of them" is an enumerated list before code). Lists are used for enumerations of cases ("All the three values ... Only the value name is sent"); tables absent; figures referenced constantly in ch06 ("Here is a visual explanation of what happened:") and absent in ch07. Notes/tips appear as sentences ("A useful precision to end the subsection with:", "Before closing this subsection, I would add this tip:", "Remember:"). Facts are unsourced and loosely dated ("Several years ago, in 2010", "Alain Kay"—misspelt, "Closure" listed twice); "I picked from the Ring documentation" is the one source credit. Honesty markers: "Let's say these are 99% of the cases. But the other 1%", "This is not new to you though", "I won't go further here".

**7. Three exemplary passages**

- § Object scope: "Ring obeys and knocks at that function door in line 5. Immediately, a local scope is activated ... Nevertheless, inside the show() room, we are still visible, me and you, because we are super globals!"
- § Beyond objects, declarative programming: "Here in Sousse, where I live, a beautiful touristic town in Tunisia, I help buying what we need at home to make a delicious Couscous."
- § Tracing variables: "Execute your code and, instead of admitting the final result without questioning, ask questions of what happened, how, and in which order!"

Weakest, § Objects, and object orientation: "Objects can inherit from parent objects their core data and behavior, while traversing their own personal legend, by forging specific data and operations. Meanwhile, they keep an open eye on their privacy..." — a paragraph of stacked metaphors (legend, privacy, neighbours, dancing, theatre, "chorography") with no code, where every other section earns its figure with output.

**8. What an AI would get wrong**

Easy to fake badly: (a) the exclamation marks and "isn't it?" tags, which without the small-program-then-error rhythm read as cheerleading; (b) the metaphors, which an imitator would sustain and polish, whereas he spends one and moves on; (c) the personal sample data, which becomes cute if invented rather than true (his name, his age, his town). Hard-to-fake core: the willingness to let the reader run a broken one-line program and read Ring's error message as the teacher's voice ("let Ring tell us"), repeated nine times without boredom; and the habit of saying where he stops ("I'll leave it for later", "homework") instead of pretending completeness.
