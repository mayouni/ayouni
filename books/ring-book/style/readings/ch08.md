# Close reading — chapter 8, "Designing games in Ring"

Read 2026-10-11 by a fresh reader (an agent given the text and `STYLE-BRIEF.md`, nothing else). ~20,000 words; ~1,050 code lines; 23 figure captions; 14 reference tables. Headings cited as written in the file.

**1. Shape of a chapter**

Opening, three moves: (a) a state-of-the-field survey with named products and a claim of uniqueness ("to the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of any initiative", the opening); (b) a promise framed as "three concerns", each starting "I will" ("One: I will help you understand the conceptual foundations"); (c) a roadmap that names every section by number and title, then a personal hook: "let me tell you... my personal story". The roadmap also pre-answers an objection: "why did I choose game programming as a final chapter".

Sections: short abstract paragraph, then either a code-then-commentary loop ("Execute and observe the result…", § Setting the ambiance) or a reference dump (§ Game Engine: nine "The next table presents..." tables with a "Reminder: this X class inherits" line). Each subsection ends with a hand-off sentence promising the next: "so stay connected", "Wait for the section ... to get the answer", "Let's end up the section with the last skill".

Close: no summary, no exercises list. Instead a cultural essay (§ Beyond gaming, video games culture) citing a TED talk and a book, a bridge to the next chapter, and a release: "you can leave the book now: all you need to be a good programmer in Ring been acquired."

Code placement: listing first, explanation after, nearly always ("Try it and get a window", § Opening a window, then "The most important things to keep in mind"). Listings are 10–40 lines; § A complete game in Ring is ~400 lines in 17 titled slices with "code without narration" by declared choice. Pseudocode is used for concepts (three game-loop listings in § Game Loop / § Game Timing); real code for skills.

**2. The teaching moves**

Standard sequence: show → run → observe a defect → ask → fix → name. § Setting the ambiance: "There is an odd behavior: the background is moving. How to fix it?" then sends the reader to the table ("Go upward to the parent GameObject"), then names "animate = false". A deliberate planted typo ("Tilte") teaches a language limit: "Ring won't help you by raising a syntax error".

Naming after showing: direction constants appear only after a paragraph of "If you were at center of the screen" (§ Animating objects). Definitions come as one-liners up front for concepts ("A real-time system is a program that is running inside a continuous loop", § Three fundamentals).

Reader mistakes: anticipated as a voiced objection ("You may say: it's the 1980s...", § A personal perspective; "Why this? It was not better to keep this 400 LOC long program as a downloadable", § A complete game). Bugs are left on purpose: "There is a bug here, did you remarked it?" and "the remaining bug happens when the chronometer reaches 0" (§ Implementing and testing the gameplay); "I left a subtle bug I want you to discover" (§ Before you make it, play it!).

Limits of the language/tooling: stated frankly and repeatedly, with a scope-closing formula (6 instances): "this basic chapter on game programming can't afford to cover them", "Again, this matter exceeds the scope of the chapter". Ugliness named outright: "about ten logical hierarchical levels are created, while human visual system can't afford to manage more than three" (§ Organizing and refactoring code); the IDE gap excused "from such a young project".

**3. Voice and address**

"you" 268, "we" 123, "I" 70. "I" for biography, opinion and promises ("I insist that you take it seriously"; "Personally, I think"). "we" for shared doing ("Now we build the second component"). "you, the programmer" / "you, the player" (4x) to assign roles. Imperatives are the backbone: "Execute" opens 15 sentences; "Add this", "Run and see", "Try it". Direct questions (46 "?"): rhetorical hooks ("So simple? Oh, yes."), checks ("Agreed?"), puzzles ("in what direction are you moving?"). Exclamations: 38, mostly enthusiasm or reveal ("no more!", "It's wonderful."). Grave register at the cultural close and on the digital divide: "many African rural local societies are struggling from the same problem!" Humour: dry and situational — "poor programmers" (§ Game Timing), "painting with numbers" (§ Adding a tile map), "a plate of Shakshuka (or Spaghetti code if you want)", "place your dog around the three functions" (§ Monitoring), "Happy Ringling!".

**4. The images**

Explanatory-weight metaphors: the war/peace conceit for delta time ("forge a peace charter between machine clocks and human seconds... the armistice would collapse", § Game Timing) — introduced, extended two sentences, dropped; the "building" for the engine's three layers ("On the top of the building", § Game Engine); the "corridor"/"collider" for collision (§ Detecting collisions); "bucket" of skills (title and 3 uses); "stakeholders... don't speak the same language of time". Decorative: "cosmic cultural bang", "divine revelation", "Cherry on the cake".

Cast and recurrences: the Guffa (couscous, tomatoes, onion) called back from the previous chapter; "Pick Man"; Mahmoud "the Ring designer in person" with a verbatim email; "the Ring team" and the GitHub page. Personal/cultural: Rural Youth Club, Atari 2600, teacher Muneer back from France, Science & Vie, father's MSX with a BASIC book "in Arabic", the old-book street "in the Medina of the capital Tunis", Tunisia's digital leap, Shakshuka. The story is the chapter's engine, not an aside: it justifies the chapter ("why did I choose game programming").

**5. Sentence and paragraph craft**

Long compound sentences chained with "and then"/"and thus", broken by very short verdict sentences: "Very easy.", "So easy.", "As simple as this.", "Very simple.", "It's that easy." Triads everywhere ("three concerns", "three things", "three fundamentals", "three layers", three articles). Many sentences start with "And" (22) or "But" (16). Openers: "In fact" 12, "In particular" 12, "In practice" 8, "Actually" 7, "This said"/"That's being said" 4, "Hopefully" (meaning fortunately) 4, "Let's" 23, "Now" 23. Ellipsis as section-ending suspense (15 "…"). Characteristic words: "for example" 31, "just" 33, "declarative(ly)" 30, "simple" 23, "whatever" 11, "all what" 10 (for "all that"), "hole" 9 (for whole), "staff" 7 (for stuff), "quiet" 3 (for quite), "nothing prevents you" 3, "in lieu of" 2, "expressive(ness)" 8, "consciously" 3, "real-world" 6.

Editor's cuts: stale cross-references ("page 17", "section .5", "subsection 3.3", "chap9", "Ten" vs "Twelve practical skills"); "Hopefully" misused four times; words dropped mid-sentence ("the game loop not the luxury every object"); over-long sentence at § A personal perspective ("The most valuable addition... one hand... other hand"); "very" 19 and "simple/simply" 29 as reassurance; repeated "the next table presents" 14 times.

**6. Structure of explanation**

Concrete-to-general by default: run a listing, observe, then "Take this as a rule of thumb" / "More generally, any constraint or rule or logic... must go inside the state function" (§ Intercepting events). Principle first only in the foundations sections. Tables as reference, flagged honestly: "Some of the information provided here are obvious, and some aren't clear enough." Figures as "a mental/visual approach" to algorithms. Numbered test recipes (§ Implementing and testing the gameplay). Facts: dated and named where biographical (1985, Jim Gregory, O'Malley), cited by title when cultural (Bavelier TED talk, Muriel & Crawford with a long quote); technical numbers worked by hand ("512 / 13 = 30.38px" — miscomputed vs the code's 39.38). Honesty markers: "I'm not sure of the answer", "by experience", "I guess", "my programming mind is quiet basic", "based on my several years of programming hardcoded games".

**7. Three exemplary passages**

- § A personal perspective: "This was disappointing, since we just own an Atari 2600 in our rural club. But this encouraged me to look for several other copies of the magazine and learn programming without a computer, just by imagining myself as being the computer!"
- § Game Timing: "You would think that deltaTime would be a magic solution to forge a peace charter between machine clocks and human seconds. Unfortunately, the armistice would collapse anytime you resort to a military alliance with a physics library"
- § Adding a tile map: "put every image at the corresponding block by specifying its number: 1 for fbwall.png, 2 for tbwallup.png, 3 for fbwalldown.png, and 0 for nothing. It's not programming anymore, its painting with numbers"

Weakest, § Intercepting events: "Being asynchronous and totally independent from the actual object from wich the event has emanated, this latter process will execute every function in a parallel process so the game is never blocked." Reason: asserts internals he then says you "shouldn't care about", in abstract nouns, with no code or figure to check it against — the one place the concrete-first habit lapses.

**8. What an AI would get wrong**

Easy to fake badly: (1) the rhetorical questions and "Very easy." verdicts, which here are earned by an immediately runnable listing and go hollow without one; (2) the Tunisian colour, which here is never ornament — each memory carries an argument (isolation → curiosity, the MSX book → learning by doing). Hard-to-fake core: the willingness to leave real defects in the reader's hands (planted typo, two unfixed bugs, a bug in the final game, a posted question he cannot answer) and to say plainly where the tool and the chapter stop. An imitation will fix the bugs and smooth the limits.
