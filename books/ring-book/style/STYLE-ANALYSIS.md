# How Mansour Ayouni writes — a study of *Beginning Ring Programming*

Written 2026-10-11 from the author's last Word text of the nine chapters, the annex and the front matter; the published proofs of chapters 1, 5 and 9; the 2019 proposal and story; seven close readings by readers who had only the text (`readings/`); the copyedit comparison (`copyedit-diff.md`); and the counts (`metrics.md`). Every claim below points at one of those; where it is my own reading I say "I".

The purpose is not to praise the book. It is to say which habits are the author's own, which are noise a good editor removes, and which an imitation would get wrong, so that the AYOUNI books written with agents keep the first, drop the second and know the third. The framework built on this study is `../../writing-framework/`.

## 1. What he says he is doing, and what he does

He says it the same way in five documents: Ring is "used in this book more as a computational thinking medium than a programming language"; the book is "gentle yet rigorous, with clarity as a first concern, rich visualizations and a lot of practical code examples"; it is "not a clinical user manual" but "a passionate alchemy of technical knowledge, real world programming experience and critical personal reflections"; and visuality is "an obligatory passage to write beautiful code". The stakes are set high and civilizational from the first page of the proposal: Al-Jam, Zaytoona and the cathedral of Tunis against "a messy ocean of low quality code"; programming as "a personal legend, a destiny".

What he does matches the claim in three of its four parts. The medium is the code (every section runs); the practice is real (his own projects, his own data); the visuality is doctrine (a figure deck per chapter, a diagram before the code of chapter 5, a hand-drawn world in chapter 9). The fourth part, "clarity as a first concern", is the one the record contradicts: the editor rewrote one sentence in four, and the readers found the same tics in every chapter. Clarity was the intention; the sentences needed a second hand. That matters for the framework: the second hand can now be an agent's, and it must know exactly what to touch.

## 2. The habits that are his (keep them)

These are patterns seen in three chapters or more, or once in a place that matters, and that none of the readers could ascribe to convention.

**H1. Run it, then read it.** The unit of teaching is a small program the reader runs, then its output or error quoted exactly, then one or two paragraphs that read the output back line by line. "Execute" and "Run" open fifty sentences; "Let's" fifty-five. The machine's message is treated as the teacher's voice: a one-word program `me` so that "Ring complains and says:"; nine successive compiler errors in the Guffa walkthrough, "each read as a hint" (ch 7); "Listening to Ring when she complains" as a section title (ch 1). Code comes before the explanation nearly always; principles are extracted after, with "The rule here is". Where he lapses (the object-orientation paragraph of ch 7, the event internals of ch 8, the opening of ch 2) the readers found the weakest prose of the book: abstract nouns with no run beneath them. The habit is not "examples first"; it is *the reader at the keyboard, and the machine's answer as evidence*.

**H2. Make it fail first, and leave a defect in the reader's hands.** He stages the wrong version before the right one (three failing web pages in ch 4; the happy path built before any ELSE in ch 5), rejects *correct* code on grounds of taste ("I would reject an equivalent version… just because the golden happy path rule is broken", ch 5), and ends sections by handing over something unfinished and saying so: a planted typo ("Tilte") and two unfixed bugs in ch 8, "Correct it please" in ch 2, "this is your penalty" in ch 3, "As a homework, I want you to add an apply(f) method" in ch 7, a refusal to finish the second chart ("It depends on the strategy you adopt"). Every reader named this as the hard-to-fake core: an imitation fixes the bugs.

**H3. He says where he stops.** "Exceeds the scope of this book" (six times in ch 8 alone); "I won't go further here"; "I let you dig the subject in Ring documentation"; "I'm not sure of the answer"; "That's my point of view, not a scientific truth" (ch 9, fn 25); "I didn't find a window to talk about this, may be in a future book" (annex); the limits of the language said plainly ("8-bit strings… not suitable for Arabic", "a dangerous feature a programmer should use with a sense of responsibility"). The Softanza book's seventh element, *the limit*, and the Refine book's "honest about scope" are this habit made structural.

**H4. The true particular.** Personal and local material is everywhere and it is *data*, not decoration: `me = "Mansour"`, `age = "43"`, the daughters' ages and names in a list, the wife's sentence as a string, Sousse, Regueb, the Gafsa millstone "still in use in my rural village", the cousin's 800 m medal "today August 28th, 2019", the MSX and the Arabic BASIC book, the father's ring in the dedication, the family as co-authors of Ringorialand (map by Teeba, characters by Haneen, the song by Cherihen). Geography is Africa-first (Cairo, Timbuktu, Niamey, Senegal's independence day, the six North-African countries with sourced populations), and the languages are used without explanation: "Barka da zuwa", "Taya murna", "yalla", "bienvenue", "oplà", Shakshuka for spaghetti code, Quran 25:53 for the two seas. The readers' verdict is unanimous: each memory carries an argument (the magazine with no computer → learning by imagining oneself the computer), and the colour would become kitsch the moment it is invented rather than true. The editor queried the Hausa aside and the apples and kept them.

**H5. One metaphor, spent and dropped; a few that carry the load.** Most metaphors are home-made, live one sentence, and are abandoned without ceremony: scopes as weather, the debugger as "your spy's eye", `eval()` as "the horse of all functions", delta time as a peace charter that collapses "anytime you resort to a military alliance with a physics library". A handful are sustained because they explain what no code there explains: the Drakon happy path ("the further to the right, the worse it is"), the clay of the craftsman, the house whose window you do not enter by, the Guffa that becomes the running example, and the whole water–sand–millstone–lights mapping of Ringorialand. He theorises the practice: chapter 9 hunts the definition of metaphor through Aristotle, Cicero, Elvis and the Arabic *majāz mursal* before giving his own, and `materials/fiction.rtf` is his reading note on metaphor in fantasy writing. He also attacks the industry's ruling metaphor (construction) and proposes another (the game). The habit, then, is deliberate: metaphor is a tool he picks up, uses, and puts down, and when he keeps one he maps it term by term.

**H6. The address: I, we, you, and the tool as a person.** "You" is the reader at the keyboard (1,760 times), "we" the pair reading the output together (1,060), "I" the witness and the opinion (520): "In my case, it was:", "Personally, I would just use @", "Here, I really need your attention, because I'm unveiling my own way of thinking". The language is addressed as a person: "Let's ask Ring a question", "she complains", "Ring obeys and knocks at that function door". Questions are beats, not rhetoric: "Agreed?", "Tired? I understand.", "You see?", "Practice? Yes, nothing is better than practice." Exclamations (140) sit after a run or a reveal. The editor turned the roadmap "we" into "I will / you will", neutered Ring's "she", and left the beats and the exclamations alone.

**H7. The rhythm: long, then short.** A long cumulative sentence (often a triad: "pragmatic, realistic and free"; "kind in making a sequence, hero in taking decisions, but very weak in repeating") followed by a verdict of one to four words: "Same result.", "So natural.", "Very easy.", "Nothing really." Almost half the paragraphs are one sentence. The colon is the signature mark (15 per 1,000 words); the em dash is nearly absent (0.5). Sentences begin with "And" or "But" one time in twenty. Repetition as emphasis: "negotiate, and negotiate, and negotiate!", "play and play again".

**H8. Counted sets with names.** Three commandments ("Clean Order", "One Start, One End", "Clean Logical Parts"), the Gang of Four, nine things, twelve skills, seven steps, four commandments of Ringaros, Table Complete. Nine itself is such a number. The named rule is how a principle is made portable; the count is how a chapter promises its value.

**H9. The roadmap and the bridge.** Every chapter opens with what it will do, section by section, in the first person; every section ends with a hand-over to the next ("so stay connected", "from a programming garden full of function-flowers, to a programming by object-stars…"); chapters end on a bridge, a release ("you can leave the book now"), a homework, or a personal coda (the volleyball title and the cousin's medal), almost never on a summary (Apress added the "Summary" headings).

**H10. The stakes are lifted.** Each chapter, at least once, rises from the technical point to a claim about programming, people or civilisation: freedom and its price (ch 2), the cornerstone of strong skills (ch 5), the digital divide (ch 8), master against good programmer, "programming is providing lights to someone in need" (ch 9). This is the trait the editor flagged hardest ("these seem like some big statements that aren't backed up") and the one the author kept.

**H11. The questioner.** In the annex he asks in volleys of three or four, runs the test himself ("I took 5 minutes this morning… a gain of 72%"), reports the numbers, concedes in public, and thanks in superlatives. The same stance built the Softanza book's record: the study of the record, the rulings, the requests to the library.

**H12. Ambition, then the cut.** The 2019 plan had thirty chapters in five parts; the V2 table of contents nineteen; the book nine in three parts and an annex, with chapter 9 as the surviving ambition (99 pages, almost no code, 56 figures, 67 footnotes). He plans large and ships what runs.

## 3. The noise (cut it, and let a tool do the cutting)

These are the habits the editor corrected most and the readers listed as tics in every chapter. None of them carries meaning; all of them are what an agent removes best.

- **Calques from French and homophones:** *hole* (whole), *staff* (stuff), *witch* (which), *fellow* (follow), *then* for *than*, "from one hand… from the other hand", "in the same time", "obeys to", "a useful information", "in plus of", *quiet* for *quite*, "yet" used as "and" ("gentle yet rigorous", "very nice answer, yet really instructive").
- **Intensifiers and reassurance:** very (58), really, simply (31), just (120), "dead simple", "obvious", "As simple as that!", "Very easy." when the listing has not yet run.
- **Stacked hedges and apologies:** "maybe, and probably, and hopefully, and some times, and many times…"; "I agree this is an exaggeration. I'm sorry."
- **Trailing ellipses and colon lead-ins to an unnamed figure** ("like this:", "Result:", "…") — twelve of twelve ellipses became periods in chapter 1.
- **Superlatives and self-advertising:** "one of the most instructive chapters of the entire book", "maybe for the first time" (three times), "unprecedented", "the root cause of all problems in software".
- **Scope inflation in the promises:** "How to contribute the official Ring codebase" as a thing learned in chapter 1.
- **Run-on roadmaps** (seven sentences of section numbers), repeated full listings, "the next table presents" fourteen times.
- **Fact slips and drift:** stack overflow for numeric overflow, "Alain Kay", a miscomputed division, Ringopus/Ringapus and storz/strorz, "five groups" over a table of seven, 10 million against 22 million.
- **Emojis** (the editor refused them three times; the Refine book's rule 11 already bans them).

## 4. Where the editor was wrong, or where only the author can decide

The copyedit also removed things that the readers counted as voice. These are not noise; they are the author's calls, and the framework must not decide them for him:

1. **Ring as "she".** Four times in chapter 1, neutered to "it" everywhere. The personification is his (H6); the gender is a choice.
2. **The reader as "she/her" and "craftsman".** The editor asked for "craftsperson"; he kept "craftsman" and the feminine reader. The AYOUNI rule since 2026 is "crafter" (CRAFTER-01), which settles the noun; the pronoun of the reader is still his.
3. **The flourishes:** "Ready? Please give me your hand…", "What the hell is this?!", "Mooooving so smoothly…", "furious relationship with programming", "inviting fantasy to the programming desert, many palms arose". The editor cut them; two readers called them the truest sample of the voice.
4. **The narrator "we" in roadmaps.** Changed to "I will / you will". The Softanza brief already chose "I" and "you".
5. **"Ringuify", "skewer", the apples, the Karni aside:** queried, kept. Right.

## 5. What an imitation gets wrong

The seven readers answered this independently and converged:

- *Easy to fake badly:* the exclamation and the "isn't it?" tag (hollow without the run that earns them); the Tunisian and Arabic colour (kitsch when invented, or generic when chosen by a model); the metaphors (an imitation sustains and polishes what he spends and drops); the counted sets (a listicle); the footnote jokes and apologies (overdone in a page); the calques (either caricatured or scrubbed).
- *Hard to fake:* leaving a real defect in the reader's hands; saying plainly where the tool and the chapter stop; rejecting correct code for a reason of taste and giving the reason; the true particular (his name, his age, his town, his daughters, a date "today"); the stance of the questioner who ran the test and reports the number; lifting the stakes from real conviction and then owning the overclaim.

The last point is the one that matters most for books written with agents. The Softanza verification of October 2026 found 624 statements to narrow in 41 pages, almost all of them a design told as built, a guard credited with more than it proves, a duration or a "first" the record did not give. That is H10 without H3: the stakes lifted and the stop not said. The agents inherited the author's reach and not his concessions, because the concessions live in the moment ("I agree this is an exaggeration") and the reach lives in the plan. The framework's job is to make the concession structural (the court, the limit, the loss beside the win) so that the reach can stay.

## 6. The two later books against this study

- *Refinement-Oriented Programming* (2026) chose "documentary, calm, grounded, plain, honest about scope", em dashes as the workhorse, no exclamation outside speech, a fictional methodologist "never named". It kept H3 and H8, dropped H4 (the particulars are a composed bank's), H6 (no I), H7's colon for the dash, and H10's open conviction. It is well written and it is not his voice; its CLAUDE.md calls it "the agent's rhythm" without saying so.
- *Softanza* (2026) chose first person, "you", plain words for a non-native reader, examples before explanations, the limit as the seventh element, "the loss beside the win", "warmth in the stories, never in adjectives", one exclamation per chapter, and `[AUTHOR: …]` markers rather than an invented memory. It kept H1 (the court is "run it, then read it" made law), H3, H6's I/you, H8 (nine elements, six doors, three stage words), H9; it rationed H7's exclamations and H10's stakes; and it left 77 holes where H4 should be, which were then filled by policy with "the record holds none". The French edition and the verification phases are H11 at scale.

Both books are more disciplined than the Ring book and both are colder. The framework that follows is written to get the discipline and the warmth in the same page: the discipline from what the agents do well (run, count, verify, translate, keep the shape), the warmth from what only he can give (H4, H5's choices, H10's conviction, the decisions), and the joints between them written down.
