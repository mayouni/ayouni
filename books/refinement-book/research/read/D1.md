## 12-what-the-auditor-saw.md

1. **What I remember.** The auditor's question has three parts: who approved, what it affected, how you know the letters matched. The library answers the first from a saved governance file, as *who acted and under what authority at the time*. It answers the second in part and the third only by pointing at a rule in the code. The proof is the save and load round trip, three decisions read back. The limit is plain: no timeline, no approver, no before/after values and no rule kept in the file.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under "The record that survives": "Here is the change of chapter 11 once more". It is not quite the same change. Chapter 11 declared two rules (`cap-within-regulation` and `letter-covers-cap`); this block declares only the second. A teacher who sets the two blocks side by side will be asked why a rule disappeared. Either say "with one of its two rules", or keep both.
   Under "What it affected": chapter 6's exercise ends "Keep it; chapter 12 returns to it." Chapter 12 does not return to the reader's hand-written cascade. It only says "The cascade of chapter 6 is asked of a point today; it is not kept with the decision." The promise is not kept. The easiest fix is a sentence here that sends the reader to the list they kept, or a change to the sentence in chapter 6.
3. **Where I doubted.** Under "Outside the story": "It uses the name Softibank for that bank too, and says the name is fictional." Chapter 1 says Softibank is invented. Here the same name also stands for a real, delivered bank, under its own pseudonym. The page keeps the two apart with care. A class will still ask "so was it real?", and the answer takes a paragraph. I note it for the author (the name is his ruling) and do not ask for a change.
   Under "What I refused": "A record a person writes afterwards is missing the week it is needed." This is aphoristic, and nothing on the page shows it. It is acceptable as a verdict line.
4. **Where it moved me.** Under "The court": "the file records what was true, not what is." That is the chapter's reveal, and the one line I would put on a slide. "That is fiction, and I mark it as such." Refusing the clean audit report its weight is the book at its most honest. Where it bored me: the second paragraph of "Outside the story" restates the absent items that the limit then lists again.
5. **The code.** I understood both blocks from the sentence before each. The output matches the promise: two files, then 3 decisions, `compliance-officer`, `autonomous`, `3`, and 2 decisions for the pricing agent. The text explains why the time is not printed. The exercise (`? read(cFile)`) depends on `cFile` from the first block, so it should say so ("after the block above"). Cut nothing.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** The headings are in order. The dates hold: September plus eighteen months gives March, and the auditor's "The September before last" is right from March. The front matter says the governance carries "the authority and risk at the time" and the block shows it. Apart from the two rules and the chapter 6 promise above, I found nothing.
7. **The door.** These pages carry no door lines, so I came in as the educator. For a teacher, the three-part reading of the auditor's question under "What the record answers, and what it does not" is the most teachable passage of Part IV. A class can grade the library against the question part by part.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`. Make the "once more" block honest about the rule it drops, and keep chapter 6's promise.

## 13-the-environment-specified.md

1. **What I remember.** Refine is the product built on the library. It is specified in over a hundred documents, drawn as an interactive prototype, and started as a command whose `version`, `status` and `init` run and whose six other verbs answer that they are queued. The gate stays the library's. Three parts of the specification answer this book's limits: twelve transactional primitives, the interface and data domains, and live refinement.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under "What runs today": "The command is its own ledger, and it reads the same one this paragraph does." I read it three times. I think it means the command reports its milestones from the same roadmap file the chapter cites, but the sentence does not say so.
   Under "What is specified": "(its section 5)". "Its" could be the graph model's specification or Refine's whole specification. The exercise then cites "sections 5 and 6" with no document name.
3. **Where I doubted.** Under "What runs today": "I ran it on 15 October 2026, and it answered so." This is the one built claim in the chapter with no answer printed and no way for a reader to rerun it, because the limit says there is no public repository. Elsewhere the book always shows the answer. Quote the queued verb's reply once, or say plainly that the run is the author's and cannot be repeated by the reader yet.
   Under "What runs today": "Refine's own `validate` is specified as a pipeline of four stages, without the library's governance and trust checks". This leaves a teacher with a question the page does not answer: does Refine's gate judge less than the library's? The principle says the gate is the library's, so one more clause is needed (for example, that Refine calls the library's five checks), or a statement of the gap as stage.
   Under "The limit": the site's word "specification" sits beside a command that runs. That is fine as written, but a reader may read it as a contradiction.
4. **Where it moved me.** "A screen can draw these answers well. It does not need to make them." This is the principle of the part in two short sentences. "If Refine did not exist, every block in this book would run as it did." Where it bored me: "What is shown". It is short, but it repeats "not screenshots of one in use", which "What I refused" says again.
5. **The code.** The one block is clear from the sentence before it. The refusal and the admission match chapter 5's form. The exercise is fenced `haro exercise` but contains only comments, so it is a design exercise and not a run. That works, but "Write yours before you read theirs" asks the reader to read specifications that the limit says are not public. A teacher cannot set this exercise as written. Give the answer's shape here, or point to where the specifications can be read.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** The twelve primitives count correctly (three node, three edge, three refinement and three pipeline operations). The front matter's "interactive prototype" agrees with the body's "prototype", but "working mockups" (live modes) and "drawn as a mockup" (code live mode) bring in a second word for a related thing. One word, or a clause that tells the two apart, would help. Chapter 11's absent item says the interface and data points are "not in the library's design documents", and this chapter says Refine specifies them. That is consistent (library against product), but the difference is easy to miss, so one clause saying it would help a class.
7. **The door.** As the educator: the stage triad (what runs, what is specified, what is shown) is a model a teacher can reuse for any product claim. That alone was worth the walk.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`. The exercise points to documents the reader cannot open, and the one run of the chapter is not shown.
