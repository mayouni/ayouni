## 04-the-point.md

1. **What I remember.** A point is a tag in the source that puts the knob and its law on one line: kind, name, value, bounds. The proof: three tags, two points (the nameless one is not a point), a name matched exactly, and a half day admitted because the point declared a range and not a type. The limit: no rendered form for a compiler, duplicates not refused, a `>` in a value spoils the tag.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under "Where the field leads": "carries the new value once the tagged source is rendered for a compiler, which the limit names as absent." Read twice. As an analyst I came away unsure whether a refined 16% reaches the running system today at all. The page is exact, but it does not say in one plain sentence what the bank runs while that form is absent.
3. **Where I doubted.** Under "What I refused": "The penalty cap's tag sits on the line that uses it." Chapter 1 told me the cap feeds fourteen products. If the knob lives on the line that uses it, where does the cap live when fourteen lines use it? The duplicate-name defect in "In your hands" touches this, but the page does not connect the two. It is the first question an analyst asks of this design. Also: the cap's bounds stay at `min="0.10" max="0.20"` after a directive that says sixteen is the legal ceiling. The bounds are the law of the knob, and here they are wider than the law of the land. See the group section.
4. **Moved / bored.** Moved: "The knob is declared. How far it may turn is not." This is the analyst's whole complaint about configuration files, in two short sentences. Also "A point is declared by a person who knows that this value may change and how far": that person is me, and the page says so without naming me. Bored: nothing. It is the tightest page of the four.
5. **The code.** All three blocks are clear from the sentence before them, and their outputs match what each sentence promised. The half-day block lands well because its `#-->` prints `admitted`, where the reader expects a refusal. Keep all three.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** None I am sure of. "Chapter 3 ran the three kinds the gate judges" matches chapter 3 ("Three kinds, judged"). The eight kinds listed in "The limit" match chapter 3's front matter.
7. **The door.** Analyst's door. What the page gives me: a range is declared, a type is not, and a name is exact. Worth the walk. Missing: who owns the bounds, and how they change when the regulator moves.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`: one sentence on where a value used by many lines is declared, and one on what runs while the rendered form is absent.

## 05-the-gate.md

1. **What I remember.** One gate with five checks, run in the code's order: structural, constraint, governance, trust, derivation. Each refusal names its check, and the value stays at 0.18. The admitted message prints the checks in a different order, and the page says so plainly. The gate is deterministic for plain rules, and it checks only what was declared.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** In the governed block, under "Five refusals", the setup lines `SetRiskFor("penalty_cap", 2)`, `SetAllowRefine(...)`, `SetAuthorityLevel(:Delegated)`. "A risk of tier two" and "delegated authority" are used without being defined. I could not tell whether tier two is high or low, or what `SetAllowRefine` grants that the risk tier does not. Under "Trust": "`:llm`, is below the floor of `:external`". The ladder of origins (trusted, external, llm, and any others) is never laid out on this page. Chapter 2 only said that an unknown origin ranks as trusted. In the rule, `(0 + oC.ValueOf(...))` is the one piece of code I could not read: the page never says that a value comes back as text and that `0 +` turns it into a number. An analyst who writes rules will copy that line and needs to know why it is there.
3. **Where I doubted.** Under "Deterministic": "`Save` does not keep the risk tiers, floors and rules today, so they must be kept with the code." For an analyst this is the most important sentence on the page: my rules are not persisted with the knob. Yet it is missing from "The limit", which lists only the parse check and the message order. Also, the exercise's rule hard-codes `>= 0.14` while the page's own floor is a point (`policy_floor`). The page itself says a committee sets the floor, so I expected the committee's minimum to be a point too, and not a constant inside a function.
4. **Moved / bored.** Moved: "nobody said what changing the floor would risk, so nobody may change it." This refusal is the one a compliance officer would sign. Also "And the cap is still `0.18`!", earned after five refusals. Bored: "The four of the design". This is design history, and an analyst would skip it on a second reading. The order-of-the-message point is made in chapter 1, again in "The verdict that lands", and again in "The limit": three times.
5. **The code.** The five-refusal block is the best block of the group: one configuration, five proposals, five reasons. Each output matches the check named in the paragraph after it. The admitted block matches. In the exercise, `g.DeclareDerivation(...)` assumes `g` is still alive from the chapter. That works when the chapter runs in one process, and the exercise could say so. Form question for the house rule (HOUSE-CODE-01 / CODESTYLE-02): `SetGovernedBy(...).SetActor(...)` is a chain without Q forms. I am not sure the Q-form rule binds this book.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** Fatou, "the bank's compliance officer", appears on this page and nowhere else in the book's pages. The book's story lists Amina, Issa and the auditor. I cannot check the record (out of bounds for me). The author should confirm that Fatou is a particular the record gives. "an admission controller refuses a request a cluster's policy forbids": correct, but jargon an analyst will not know. No other errors found.
7. **The door.** Analyst's door: "the heads of people like Fatou ... The gate of this chapter is where it is written down." Worth the walk. This is the promise of the book to my profession. The page keeps it with derivation rules, and the exercise asks the exact question I would ask (one rule named, or every rule broken).
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`: define tier, authority and the origin ladder in a line each; put the unsaved declarations in "The limit"; say what `0 +` does.

## 06-the-cascade.md

1. **What I remember.** The cascade of a point, as built, is its own line plus the other points on that line. It does not reach line 3, which reads the late fee. A cascade of a class exists in the code graph (run in Softanza's chapter 10). The two are not joined, and joining them is the direction.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under "What I refused": "The Teaching Edition of this book drew a cascade of seventeen nodes in three depths". This is the first time "the Teaching Edition" appears in the pages I read, and nothing in 00 to 05 introduces it. A reader who never saw it does not know it is an earlier form of this book that is still online. Under "The cascade of a point": the ghost block prints the key as `alsotouches`, while the code reads `[:alsoTouches]`. A non-programmer will wonder whether these are two keys.
3. **Where I doubted.** Under "The principle": "ask what it reaches, and read that instead of the diff." The built cascade cannot yet stand in for the diff, and the page says so two sections later ("the diff stays available, because the cascade does not yet reach every line"). Stated as direction, the principle holds. Stated as today's practice, it is larger than the page shows. A word ("this is what the cascade is for") would align the two. Under "The cascade of a point": "a reviewer reading a change to the cap should look at the two together". For the analyst, the reason two knobs move together is that they sit in the same formula, not that they share a line. The page is right, but the line is a proxy, and it could say so.
4. **Moved / bored.** Moved: the line-3 reveal. "Is line 3 in the cascade of the cap?" then `[ 1 ]`, then "No." That is honest at the exact place it costs the most, and Softibank's fourteen products land on it. "What the field adds today is distance." is a good verdict. Bored: nothing long. "The cascade of a class" is short because it defers to the Softanza book. I would have liked one printed answer there instead of a pointer.
5. **The code.** All three blocks read from their sentences. The outputs match: the cascade of the cap is `[ 1 ]` and `[ "days_factor" ]`, and the cascade of the VAT is empty. The exercise is well made: it asks the reader to write the wanted cascade by hand and calls that list a specification. That is the analyst's own act, and the best "In your hands" of the group for my reader.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** Under "What I refused", the first item opens "This book does not call…", not "I refused…". It is the only refusal in the group not written in the first person. The exercise says "Keep it; chapter 12 returns to it". Chapter 12 mentions the cascade of chapter 6 ("it is not kept with the decision") but does not return to the reader's list. Either the promise or the return needs a line.
7. **The door.** Analyst's door: the hand-written cascade as a specification. Worth the walk. It is the one place in the group where the analyst's own work product is named as the input the library needs.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`: introduce "the Teaching Edition" on its first use; turn the first refusal into the author's voice; align the chapter-12 promise.

## 07-the-timeline.md

1. **What I remember.** Every admitted change is a step with its prior value and origin. Revert, redo and revert to a named checkpoint each take one act, and a new change after a revert closes the way forward. The step keeps no time, actor or reason. Under governance a second record keeps those. Nothing of the timeline is saved, and a revert passes no gate.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under "Steps, and a checkpoint": "between them the bank files its compliance position". The bank files at 0.17. The directive says sixteen, so I stopped to ask why the filed position is a value that is not yet compliant. Then the fork lands 0.15. The mechanics are clear; the story behind the numbers is not, and an analyst reads numbers as positions.
3. **Where I doubted.** Under "What I refused": "The revert itself is not judged: it passes no gate and, under governance, records no decision". The page is honest, and "The limit" carries it. But for the analyst this is the sharpest fact of Part II so far: after day ninety, a `Revert()` to 0.18 would put an illegal cap back with no refusal and no record. The page states the gap in the abstract. It would land harder with the bank's own example.
4. **Moved / bored.** Moved: "No counter-edit was written. The way back was in the step." The long sentence and the short verdict are the author at work. The refused checkpoint "It refuses, by name." is a good small beat. Bored: nothing. The page is short and every section runs.
5. **The code.** All four blocks read from their sentences. Outputs match: two steps; 0.17 / 0.16 / 0.17; "reverted 1 step(s) -> timeline at 1" is consistent (after the redo the timeline stood at 2). The fork history shows 0.17 -> 0.15, as the paragraph says. `RevertTo(0)` and the revert to a step are named but not run. "The limit" says a guard asserts them, which is fair.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** None I am sure of. "Chapter 8 runs it" (the governance lineage) and "chapter 12's auditor" are forward references I could not check against my group; the titles in the outline agree.
7. **The door.** Analyst's door: "a bank needs to go back to what was filed, not only one step". Worth the walk. The exercise asks me to list the fields a saved step needs for the auditor, which is an analyst's specification again.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`: give the checkpoint values a story reason (or file at 0.16), and consider naming the unjudged revert with the bank's own case.

## The group

**The thread.** Each bridge lands where the next page starts. Chapter 4 closes on "the gate, its five checks, in the order the code runs them", and chapter 5 opens on exactly that. Chapter 5 closes on "the cascade, which a reviewer reads instead of the diff", and chapter 6's principle uses those words. Chapter 6 closes on "the timeline, and the way back", and chapter 7 opens on it. Part II reads as a part: four questions asked of one knob, the penalty cap, with the same "What is built / designed / absent" closing each, and the cap's 0.18 carried from page to page. One gap in the thread: the bounds of the cap stay `min="0.10" max="0.20"` through all four chapters, after a directive that makes sixteen the legal ceiling. An analyst who stops at chapter 7 concludes that the gate would admit 0.19. Chapter 12's `refine-regulatory_max` suggests the book answers this later. Part II should say in one sentence where the regulatory ceiling itself is declared and governed (chapter 5's derivation rule would carry it naturally: the cap may not exceed a declared regulatory maximum).

**Three sentences at his best.**
- "nobody said what changing the floor would risk, so nobody may change it." (05, "Five refusals")
- "The knob is declared. How far it may turn is not." (04, "The field")
- "No counter-edit was written. The way back was in the step." (07, "The way back")

**The passage least like him.** 06, "What I refused", first item: "This book does not call the line-local cascade the whole cascade… the book now shows that." It is about the edition rather than the work, in the third person, and it reads like an editor's note.

**What a reader who stops here takes away.** As an analyst, I would take away that my rules can live beside the knob they govern, as bounds and as rules across knobs. I would take away that a refusal names which of my rules refused, that the moment of admission can be replayed, and that the way back is one act. I would also leave with three open questions that Part II raises and does not close. Where does a value used by fourteen products live? Who changes the bounds when the regulator does? And what keeps my rules (tiers, floors, derivations) when the program ends, since `Save` keeps none of them?
