## 02-what-broke.md

1. **What I remember.** Writing became cheap and reading did not, so approval drifts from understanding; trust must rest on the change, which must be validated, scoped, attributed, reversible, explainable. One run of an AI-agent proposal shows all five; the limit is that the reason is not kept in the history.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under **In your hands**: "Then look at what the history keeps of the second change ... `Why()` answers only for the last proposal." `Why()` appears here for the first time; the blocks used `r[:why]`. The exercise code does not call it either. Add `? o.Why()` to the exercise or say what it is.
   Same section: "the second change": with `o` carried over from the block above, the rounding proposal is the second step in the history, but the instruction "propose a change to each of them, one at a time" makes "the second change" ambiguous.
3. **Where I doubted.** **Scoped** is defined as "what it reaches is computed and shown, not discovered in production", and the run shows only "what else sits on its line", `[ "days_factor" ]`. The page is honest about it in **The court** and **The limit**, but the list promises more than the run shows; the bullet could say "what it reaches, as far as the code declares it, is computed".
   "In my view" three times in **The afternoon** and the principle paragraph, plus "in the teams I see"; each is fair, together they read as hedging the chapter's central claim. One placed well would do.
4. **Moved / bored.** "The name is still under the change. It certifies less." The best two sentences of the group. And "Judging authors does not keep pace with an afternoon. Judging changes can." **The field** in this chapter repeats the shape of chapter 1's **The field** closely enough that I skimmed it on the second reading.
5. **The code.** Two blocks. The first, four points listed, answers the beat "Can a program say where it may be changed?" exactly. The second, five answers read against five properties, is the clearest teaching block in the group. The walk-through takes the properties in a different order (Validated, Explainable, Scoped, Attributed, Reversible) from the list (validated, scoped, attributed, reversible, explainable); following the block's output order is defensible, but say so or match the list. `nl` is used without a word; a newcomer to Haro will guess, but a clause costs nothing.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** "the next paragraph says so" (**Where the field leads**): the next paragraph is under a new heading, **The limit**; "the limit says so" would be exact.
7. **The door.** The programmer walks in by the second block, and it pays: one run, five properties, each pointed at a value I can see.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`: `Why()` introduced in an exercise that does not use it; the scope bullet; the property order.

## 03-the-unit-of-change.md

1. **What I remember.** A text edit sets the cap to 0.61 and the library neither refuses nor records it; the same slip as a typed proposal is refused, and the right value becomes a step. Three kinds of point are judged today (parameter, algorithm, library); a block-kind point parses and passes unchecked.
2. **Where I lost the thread.** Under **The edit the gate never sees**: `substr(cSrc, '"0.18"', '"0.61"')`. To a programmer, a three-argument `substr` is not obviously "replace"; most languages read it as substring by position. One clause, "`substr` with three arguments replaces the second by the third", and the block is clear.
   Under **The principle**: "They are not chapter 2's five properties; they are what a change must be for it to carry them." Two fives in two chapters; the sentence is needed and it works, but I read it twice.
3. **Where I doubted.** "three times the ceiling it declares" (0.61 against 0.20): "more than three times" is exact.
   The exercise tag `<R:BLOCK name="limit_rule" value="amount < 100">` puts a `<` inside a tag's attribute. **The limit** says a block point "parses and is listed"; I cannot tell from the page whether that was run with a `<` in the value. Since the exercise prints nothing, a programmer whose parser chokes will think he broke it. Worth one run by the author's side.
4. **Moved / bored.** "The point now holds `0.61`, three times the ceiling it declares, and nothing refused it!" The exclamation is earned: it is the reveal. And "The diff is what the code looks like after a change. The refinement is the change itself". Not bored.
5. **The code.** Three blocks, each announced, each output as promised. The first and second form the best pair in the group: same slip, two units, two outcomes. The third shows the option kinds plainly. **The limit** carries the most valuable sentence for a programmer: a text edit's out-of-bounds value is "a later step records it as the value to return to": that deserves a block of its own, or at least a pointer to where it is run.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** **The limit**, last paragraph: three gaps are named (unchecked kinds; the gate not re-reading the code as it stands; a non-number stopping the program), then "Both are the next steps of the gate". Three items, "Both". Front matter `built` and **The court** agree that the library kind is run and not guarded; consistent. The eight kinds listed in **The limit** are eight; consistent with the front matter.
7. **The door.** The programmer's door is the first block, the edit the gate never sees, and it is the strongest walk in the group.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`: "Both" for three, the `substr` clause, the `<` in the exercise tag.
