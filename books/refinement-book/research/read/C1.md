2. **Lost the thread.** Under "Governance", the refusal prints "(SHOULD does not cover it)". The page never says what SHOULD is. As an AI agent parsing that refusal to correct myself, I cannot map "SHOULD" to "delegated, level 2" without guessing. The four levels are listed only by name ("advisory, delegated, autonomous and emergency override"); only two get their numbers. An agent needs the full ladder, 1 to 4, to know what to ask for.
3. **Doubted.** "It is a one-line change; the book has written the request (RL-04), and the library's session has not yet received it." An internal ticket id and a report on another session's inbox, in a reader's page; the first half is enough. Under "An origin it does not know": the gap is named honestly, but from my door it is larger than the page lets it sound: any proposal saying `As(:auditor)`, or saying nothing (the default is `trusted`), clears every floor. The limit says it; the floor's section ("a proposal declared as composed by an AI model may change the display text... and may not change the penalty cap") reads as a guarantee before that.
4. **Moved.** "The authority is recorded as it was, not as it is." That is the sentence an auditor and an agent both need. And "The floor is on the knob, not on the people." Nothing bored me.
5. **Code.** All four blocks follow their sentences. The `:auditor` block's "Admitted!" with its parenthesis is right to explain the fixed check list. The record block prints exactly the four fields the text promises and says why the time is omitted.
6. **Errors.** "SHOULD" and, in chapter 11, "PERMISSION (can)" are library vocabulary the book never glosses (see the group). "RL-04" in front matter and twice in prose.
7. **The door.** The AI agent: the exercise is the best line of the group for me: "decide whether the refusal says enough for the AI agent to correct its proposal without asking a person." Worth the walk. It would be worth more if the page itself answered it once for the authority refusal.
8. **Verdict.** Ready after the items above: gloss SHOULD and the four levels, and drop the RL-04 process talk.

Chapter 10 closes Part III and names Softibank's eighteen months; chapter 11 opens on Softibank: lands. **For an AI agent, across the group.** The refusals are the best interface in the book: each names its check first and its reason after. Three things keep me from reading them unaided: library words never glossed (SHOULD, PERMISSION (can)); the authority ladder given by number for only two of four levels; and no shown way to read a point's bounds, floor and risk before proposing. A short table in the glossary, refusal prefix to what the agent should change, would make chapter 8's exercise answerable from the book.

**Three sentences that are the author at his best.**
- "A change to the code that left the letter at eighteen per cent would be a correct program and a wrong promise." (chapter 11)
- "The AI agent did not have to be told not to touch the interest rate. The surface did not include it." (chapter 10)
- "The authority is recorded as it was, not as it is." (chapter 8)

**One passage least like him.** Chapter 8, "An origin it does not know": "It is a one-line change; the book has written the request (RL-04), and the library's session has not yet received it." Process reporting, an internal id, and a word on another session's inbox, where the rest of the book says stage and direction.

**What a reader who stops here takes away.** An AI agent's proposals are judged like anyone's, in a fixed order, and the bank governs the knob, the origin and the actor separately; most of a regulatory change is rules, not intelligence; and a well-written rule decides the order of a change by itself. They also take away, if they read the limits, that the origin is what the proposal says, so the safety of Part III depends on the program that sets it.
