# Reading report, group C2: chapters 8 to 11

Reader: the decision-maker, a bank's head of IT or of compliance deciding whether to let AI agents change the bank's code. I skimmed `01-the-letter.md` first, read the four pages whole in order, then read them again with the brief's questions. I checked two cross references against `02-what-broke.md`, `05-the-gate.md` and `07-the-timeline.md`. I ran nothing.

## 08-trust-and-governance.md

1. **What I remember.** Two questions, kept apart: *who* acts (governance: permission, authority level, declared risk tier) and *from where* the proposal comes (trust: four ranked postures, a floor per point). Governance keeps a lineage that records the authority the actor held *at the time*. The limit: the origin is declared, not proven, and an origin the library does not recognise is ranked as trusted.
2. **Where I lost the thread.**
   - Under "Governance", the refusal reads `(SHOULD does not cover it)`. Nothing on this page or in chapters 1 to 7 says what SHOULD is: a level name, a keyword, an RFC-style modal? A compliance head will read the output twice and still not know.
   - Under "An origin it does not know": "the book has written the request (RL-04), and the library's session has not yet received it." "The library's session" is how the book is made, not something a reader can picture. "RL-04" is an internal code with no key on the page.
3. **Where I doubted.**
   - The page's central promise is "A floor exists to refuse what it does not recognise". Two blocks later, `:auditor` clears the highest floor. The page says this honestly. But for my reader it is the most consequential fact in the group, and it sits in a short paragraph that opens with "Admitted!". An exclamation over a gap can read as delight. Name it under its own heading, or move it to the start of "The limit".
   - "The library's governance takes the actor's name as given" (under "Where the field leads"). This is correct and important. Together with the default posture `trusted` (under "The floor of trust": "A proposal is `trusted` unless it says otherwise"), it means a proposal that declares nothing is trusted. The page never puts those two facts side by side. A bank reader will put them together, and should find the author already did.
4. **Where it moved me.** "The floor is on the knob, not on the people." And under "The record governance keeps": "what the actor was allowed to do at the time it did it." That is the sentence an auditor wants. It bored me nowhere; the page is tight.
5. **The code.** Every block follows from the sentence before it. The governance block's output matches its prose, levels 2 and 3 included. The record block is the only run of the decision fields, and the page says so ("No guard reads the fields of a decision"). That honesty is right. I would cut nothing.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** Nothing I am sure of. One form point: `SetGovernedBy(...).SetActor(...)` and `SetRiskFor(...).SetAllowRefine(...).SetAuthorityLevel(...)` chain without Q forms. If this book follows the Softanza book's CODESTYLE-02, these break it. If it does not, ignore this.
7. **My door.** The decision-maker. The "Governance" section and "The record governance keeps" were worth the walk. They answer "can I see who decided, under what authority, at the time?" with a run.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`. Define SHOULD, drop "RL-04" and "the library's session", and set the unknown-origin gap and the trusted default side by side.

## 09-the-ai-model-comes-last.md

1. **What I remember.** Most of a regulatory change is rule work, and a plain loop over the declared points does it exactly, offline, for free. The AI model is called last, for language work such as reading a circular. Its draft is only a proposal: the bounds refuse its slip, and the floor of trust refuses its corrected value when the draft is declared as the AI model's. No AI model is actually called in these runs.
2. **Where I lost the thread.**
   - Under "The principle": "The sentence is the Softanza book's chapter 17, with its words for the model and the court given as this book's". I read it three times. It is a note about translation of vocabulary, not something a reader needs. Cut it, or say "adapted from".
   - Under "The AI model, last": "as a person's proposal would be after reading the circular and agreeing with the draft". How does the program know a person read it? The default posture is trusted whoever sends it (chapter 8). This is the same hole as in chapter 8, and here it is load-bearing. The third answer in the block, `[:admitted] 1`, is the corrected value *without* `As(:llm)`. A head of compliance will ask: what stops the AI agent from simply omitting `As(:llm)`? The page's answer is under "The limit" ("the origin is what the proposal declares"), two sections later. It should sit beside the run.
3. **Where I doubted.**
   - Under "The rules first": "at no cost, offline" and "exactly right is what rules do". Fair for the loop shown. But the loop sets every point to `0.16` because the author wrote `"0.16"`. The rule that *every product with a penalty cap* takes the circular's value is not declared anywhere; the loop moves every point in the text. On a real code base with points other than caps, this loop would move them all. One sentence on selecting the points by kind or by name would close it.
   - "an AI model may suggest and may not land" is the right sentence. It is true only if the program, not the AI agent, stamps the posture.
4. **Where it moved me.** "Fluency is not a check." And "The AI model generated; the gate admitted or refused; and the person did what the gate cannot". That is the clearest division of labour in the group. Nothing bored me. "The field" is short and fair.
5. **The code.** The loop block is understood from its sentence, and its three `#-->` lines are what was promised. The draft block works: the split, the slip refused by `constraint`, the floor, the trusted admission. The exercise is good: the third draft cannot be read at all, which is the real-world case. Keep all of it.
6. **Errors of fact or form.**
   - "(Governance passes here because none is declared, as chapter 2 said.)" checks out (`02-what-broke.md`: "the other three checks pass when nothing is declared for them").
   - Chapter 19 of the Softanza book is cited for two different things: the domain language model (under "Where the field leads") and the grammar compiler (under "The limit"). Both may be true; worth one check.
7. **My door.** The decision-maker. "The AI model, last" was worth it: it is the one page a board member could be handed.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`. Move the "who stamps the posture" caveat next to the run, scope the loop, and cut the chapter 17 vocabulary note.

## 10-the-safest-write-surface.md

1. **What I remember.** Give an AI agent the declared points, not the file system. A proposal to an undeclared place is refused by name at the first check. Because a refinable code is a value, a copy is a rehearsal that leaves the original untouched. The door for AI agents (one command: rehearse, commit, grammar and the rest) is decided and not built. Today a team's own program stands in for it.
2. **Where I lost the thread.**
   - Under "The map": "the bounds are in the tags the AI agent reads with them." The printed `RefinementPoints()` shows name, kind and value, not bounds. So how does the AI agent read the bounds? A call that prints them, or one clause naming it, would settle this.
   - Under "The map": "provided the program around the code gives the AI agent `Refine` and nothing else; the timeline's `Redo`, for one, re-applies a step without the gate." This is the most important condition in Part III, and it is the tail of a long sentence. I nearly missed it. A head of IT needs it as a sentence of its own, and it belongs in "The limit" too.
3. **Where I doubted.**
   - "A bank can let an AI agent propose on it at any hour, because nothing it proposes lands without the gate". With the proviso above, and with the default trusted posture of chapter 8, this is a sentence the page proves only in part. Add the third condition: the program sets the posture, not the AI agent. Then it holds, and the reader can put a name to it.
   - Under "Where the field leads": "its application tier already serves a declared project to AI tools, read-only and audited". This is a strong built claim with no run, no chapter of the Softanza book cited, and no guard. Cite where it is shown.
   - Under "The door, decided": "On 2 October 2026 the platform ratified one command". Who ratifies, for a platform? A decision-maker reads "ratified" as a governance act and will ask by whom. "The author decided" or "the design was fixed" would be plainer, if the record supports it.
4. **Where it moved me.** "The AI agent did not have to be told not to touch the interest rate. The surface did not include it." That is the page's argument in two lines. And "I refused a door that thinks." Nothing bored me.
5. **The code.** The map block and the twin block are both understood from their sentences, and their outputs match. The exercise uses `aStep[1]`, `aStep[4]`, `aStep[3]`. The layout of `History()` was printed in chapter 7 (point, old, new, origin), so the indices are earned, but only for a reader who remembers chapter 7. "(point, old value, new value, origin)" in the lead sentence would help.
6. **Errors of fact or form.** "in the words of chapter 2. A proposal on this surface is validated, scoped, attributed and reversible". Chapter 2 has five words, with *explainable* among them. It also defines *scoped* differently: "what it reaches is computed and shown". Here *scoped* is glossed as "it names a declared point". Either keep chapter 2's five words with their meanings, or drop "in the words of chapter 2".
7. **My door.** The decision-maker. "The map" is the most persuasive section in the group for my reader. "The door, decided" is honest stage and direction.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`. Lift the `Redo` proviso into its own sentence and into "The limit", add the posture condition, cite the application-tier claim, and reconcile the chapter 2 words.

## 11-the-refinement-amina-made.md (title: "The refinement the bank made")

1. **What I remember.** The regulator's maximum is itself a point, which only the compliance officer may move. The customer's letter sits in the same refinable text as the cap, tied by a rule that the letter may not state less than the cap. The rules then force an order: cap first, letter second, regulation last. A rule of strict equality cannot be satisfied one step at a time, and the planner that would move several points together is designed, not built.
2. **Where I lost the thread.**
   - In the opening: "The record of the story gives the letter, Amina's notebook and the auditor's visit; it does not give the scenes of the weeks between, and I do not invent them." Chapter 1 told me the bank is fiction. So "I do not invent them" reads strangely to a reader: everything here is invented. "The record of the story" is the making of the book showing through. Say instead that the chapter shows the change and not the weeks, and drop the reason.
   - In the setup block, `SetActor("compliance-officer")` is followed by `SetRiskFor(...)`, `SetAllowRefine(...)` and `SetAuthorityLevel(...)`, then `SetActor("pricing-agent")` and more of the same. The prose never says that permission and authority attach to the actor last set, while risk belongs to the point whoever is set. A head of IT reading this as a configuration will misread it. One sentence before the block would fix it.
3. **Where I doubted.**
   - Under "The order the gate enforces": "with these rules, the library lets the rule move only once the code is already under it." For a compliance head this is the most arguable design call in the group. The circular *did* move first. With these rules, the bank's own record of the regulatory maximum says 18% for as long as the code lags, so the system's copy of the law is wrong for that time. The page notes the inversion from the Teaching Edition, but not what it means for a compliance officer. Is `regulatory_max` "the law" or "the bank's attested compliance"? Name which, and the order reads as a feature.
   - "Nobody had to remember that order. It was declared once, and every proposal met it." True for the run shown, and earned.
4. **Where it moved me.** "A change to the code that left the letter at eighteen per cent would be a correct program and a wrong promise." That is the best sentence in the group. "Neither can move first, so neither moves at all!" lands as a reveal. The closing line of the exercise also works: "if you find one, it is the specification of the planner". It turns a gap into a task. Nothing bored me.
5. **The code.** All three blocks are understood from their lead sentences, and every output matches the prose ("Five proposals", the PERMISSION refusal, the two derivation refusals). The `(0 + oC.ValueOf(...))` coercion is unexplained. A reader new to Haro will wonder why it is there; half a clause ("values are text; `0 +` reads them as numbers") would do.
6. **Errors of fact or form.**
   - The file and id are still `the-refinement-amina-made` while the title is "The refinement the bank made". The house may want them aligned, or may keep the id for stable links. The author should decide.
   - Under "The limit": "the equality showed what its absence costs today" is listed under *designed*. That is a comment, not a designed item; move it to the prose.
7. **My door.** The decision-maker, and the compliance head above all. "The rule, as a point" and "The order the gate enforces" were worth the walk: this is the first page where my own role, the compliance officer, appears as an actor.
8. **Verdict.** `ready after the items above`. Rewrite the "record of the story" sentence, explain how the actor setup binds, and say what `regulatory_max` stands for, so the order reads as intended.

## The group

**The thread.** Each bridge lands. Chapter 8 hands on "the program around it that would have to prove it". Chapter 9 picks up the AI agent, but does not prove the origin; it re-states in "The limit" that the origin is declared. So the bridge promises a little more than Part III delivers. Change "would have to prove it" to "must stamp it", and say where. Chapter 9's exercise points to "the door that would hold a draft to a grammar", and chapter 10 names it: a clean handoff. Chapter 10 ends with "Part III ends here. Part IV returns to Softibank", and chapter 11 opens with exactly that. Part III (9 and 10) feels like a part: the mind, then the surface. Chapter 8 closes Part II on the who-and-where question, which is right.

**The one thing the group must say plainly.** Across three pages, the safety of handing an AI agent the gate rests on three conditions, and each is stated somewhere:
- the program gives the AI agent `Refine` and nothing else (chapter 10, buried);
- the program, not the AI agent, sets the posture (stated nowhere outright);
- unknown postures are refused (absent, RL-04).

A decision-maker who reaches the end of chapter 10 needs those three in one place. Today they must assemble them.

**Three sentences of the author at his best.**
- "The floor is on the knob, not on the people." (08, "The floor of trust")
- "The AI agent did not have to be told not to touch the interest rate. The surface did not include it." (10, "The map")
- "would be a correct program and a wrong promise." (11, "The field")

**The passage least like him.** 09, "The principle": "with its words for the model and the court given as this book's". It is a note on vocabulary written for the record, not for the reader. A close second is the opening of 11 ("The record of the story gives...").

**What a reader who stops here takes away.** Govern the change, not the intelligence. Declared points are a surface an AI agent can be handed. A gate refuses by bounds, by actor, by origin and by rule, and keeps a record an auditor can read. The AI model is a proposer, called last. The reader also leaves with three honest gaps (origin declared not proven, unknown origins trusted, no multi-point planner) and one open question that should be closed: who sets the posture.
